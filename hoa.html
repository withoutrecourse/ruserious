Section 1
What Your HOA Board Just Did

Your HOA board installed automated surveillance cameras in your neighborhood — likely using your dues money, likely without a homeowner vote, and almost certainly without explicit authority in your governing documents. They entered into a multi-year contract with a private surveillance company. That company is now capturing, timestamping, and storing the movements of every vehicle in your neighborhood. Every time you leave for work, church, a doctor's appointment, or your kid's school — it is logged.

"They used your money to build a cage around your neighborhood.
Here is how you tear it down."
No
Warrant

Zero oversight. HOA surveillance cameras operate with no warrant requirement, no judicial review, and no statutory obligation to notify residents. The board signs a contract and the cameras go up. You find out when you drive past one.

Your
Dues

You are paying for it. Monthly HOA dues fund the camera contract. You are being charged to surveil yourself. That money comes from your pocket and goes to a private surveillance company under a multi-year agreement the board signed on your behalf.

No
Vote

Most governing documents don't authorize it. HOA CC&Rs written before 2018 rarely include explicit authority to install neighborhood-wide automated surveillance systems. A board that installs cameras without homeowner vote and without explicit CC&R authority may be acting outside its legal power — called ultra vires action.

Board
Liable

Board members are personally liable for ultra vires decisions in most states. Their D&O insurance has limits. They have day jobs. They are not billion-dollar companies. Sustained legal pressure from a dozen organized neighbors hits very differently than it does against a well-funded corporation.

Section 2
Legal Grounds to Challenge & Dissolve
Ultra
Vires

Board action beyond governing document authority. If your CC&Rs do not explicitly authorize the installation of automated license plate readers or neighborhood-wide surveillance systems, the board had no power to install them. Ultra vires actions by HOA boards expose individual board members to personal liability — not just the association.

Applicable in all states — check your CC&Rs for surveillance authorization language
4th
Amdt

Reasonable expectation of privacy. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable surveillance. A 2024 trial court held that a citywide ALPR network functioned as a dragnet — equating it to placing GPS trackers on every vehicle. HOA networks covering entire subdivisions raise identical constitutional concerns.

Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967) • Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018)
Common
Law

Intrusion upon seclusion. Systematic tracking of residential movement patterns without consent constitutes intentional intrusion into a private matter that a reasonable person would find highly offensive. Applies to all 50 states through common law adoption of the Restatement (Second) of Torts.

Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652B — adopted in all US jurisdictions
Breach
Fiduciary

HOA board members owe a fiduciary duty to all homeowners. Installing surveillance systems that expose homeowners to privacy violations, data breaches, and legal liability — without a vote, without disclosure, and without consent — may constitute breach of fiduciary duty. Homeowners can sue board members individually for breach of this duty.

HOA fiduciary duty recognized in all states with HOA enabling statutes
Data
Breach

Private surveillance networks have been hacked. When a surveillance company's network is breached, your movement data — where you live, when you leave, when you return, where you go — is in the hands of whoever executed the breach. The HOA board that signed that contract accepted liability for that exposure on behalf of every homeowner in the subdivision.

Board liability for data breach exposure — consult state consumer protection statutes
Dues
Misuse

Unauthorized expenditure of HOA funds. If the board signed a surveillance contract without homeowner vote and your governing documents do not authorize it, that expenditure may constitute unauthorized use of association funds. Homeowners can demand repayment and removal of board members responsible.

Varies by state HOA statute — review your state's HOA enabling act
Section 3
The Gameplan — 12 Neighbors, 30 Minutes a Week

You do not need a lawyer to start this. You need 12 motivated neighbors and 30 minutes a week. Divide the work. Rotate tasks. Document everything. The board that installed those cameras is counting on you being too busy, too isolated, and too intimidated to fight back. Twelve organized neighbors with a clear gameplan is all it takes to make their lives very uncomfortable.

1
Pair 1 — Document the Cameras

Photograph every camera location with GPS coordinates and timestamps. Note the brand, model if visible, direction of coverage, and proximity to private driveways and homes. This becomes your exhibit list. You are documenting the scope of surveillance your board installed without your consent.

2
Pair 2 — Pull the Governing Documents

Get your CC&Rs, bylaws, and any board meeting minutes from the past 3 years. You are looking for two things: (1) any language explicitly authorizing automated surveillance — if it's not there, the board acted without authority; (2) the vote or resolution authorizing the surveillance contract — if there was no homeowner vote, that is your opening.

3
Pair 3 — Publish Your Newspaper Notice

Call your county newspaper's legal notices department. Ask for the per-word rate. Submit the notice text in Section 6 of this page. Request the affidavit of publication when it runs. This establishes public constructive notice that you do not consent to data retention — and creates a court-ready sworn document tied to a specific date. Every camera capture after that date is a documented potential violation.

4
Pair 4 — Send Certified Mail to the Board

Send the formal demand letter in Section 5 via certified mail — return receipt requested — to each board member individually at their home address (public record in most states) AND to the HOA's registered agent if it has one. Keep every green card. Photograph the envelopes before mailing. This establishes actual notice to every board member personally.

5
Pair 5 — File Public Records Requests

File a written request to the HOA board demanding: (1) the full surveillance contract including term and cost; (2) all board meeting minutes where surveillance was discussed or approved; (3) the data retention policy for captured footage; (4) proof of homeowner vote authorizing the installation. They are required to provide governing documents to homeowners in most states. Denial or delay is itself actionable.

6
Pair 6 — File Individual Suits

Once your newspaper affidavit is in hand and your certified mail receipts are returned — file individually. Not class action. Individual suits filed simultaneously by multiple neighbors in the same jurisdiction give the board multiple separate legal defense obligations. Each suit requires a response. Each response costs money. Their D&O insurance has a deductible and a limit. They will feel it fast.

The rotation system: At each weekly 30-minute meeting, pairs report progress and tasks rotate. No one person carries the whole load. The board is counting on burnout. Rotation prevents it. Document every meeting with a simple date, attendees, and action items list — that record of organized sustained effort is itself evidence of good faith.

Section 6
Publish Your Newspaper Notice — The Linchpin

Most people don't know this: the local newspaper is the official public record. Courts recognize publication in a newspaper of general circulation as legally sufficient constructive notice — without argument. A Facebook post is corroborating evidence. A newspaper affidavit of publication is a sworn legal document that judges accept without question.

Call your county newspaper. Ask for the legal notices department. Ask for the per-word rate. Most county papers run $10–$25 for a short notice. Submit the text below. When it runs, request the affidavit of publication. That document is the foundation everything else is built on.

NOTICE OF PRIVACY ASSERTION — I, [YOUR FULL LEGAL NAME], a man/woman, hereby serve public notice that I do not consent to the retention of any image, data, or metadata captured from my person or vehicle by any automated surveillance or recording system installed or operated by any homeowners association, private surveillance network, or related entity. Retention beyond capture constitutes trespass against my unalienable rights. Each retention event is a separate civil action at $5,000 per incident. Full notice published at RUSERIOUS.WTF. Published for constructive notice purposes.
Replace [YOUR FULL LEGAL NAME] with your name before submitting.

After it publishes: Request the affidavit of publication. Make multiple copies. Give one to each pair in your group. File one in your evidence folder. Every camera capture of your vehicle or person after the publication date is a documented potential violation with a court-ready sworn document establishing prior notice.

Section 7
File & Win — The Evidence Package

Once your newspaper affidavit is in hand and your certified mail receipts are returned, you have a court-ready paper trail. File individually — not as a group, not as class action. Individual suits filed simultaneously by multiple neighbors create multiple separate legal defense obligations for the board. Each suit requires a separate response. Each response costs money from the D&O policy. The deductible alone will get their attention.

  • Newspaper affidavit of publication — establishes constructive notice date. The foundation.
  • Certified mail green card receipts — establishes actual notice to each board member personally.
  • Governing documents — show the board lacked authority to install surveillance.
  • Board meeting minutes — show no homeowner vote was taken.
  • Surveillance contract — shows term, cost, data retention policy, and board signatures.
  • Camera location photographs with GPS and timestamps — documents scope of surveillance.
  • Data retention evidence — FOIA response or vendor documentation showing data retained after your notice date.
  • Screenshots of RUSERIOUS.WTF — shows public constructive notice timestamp.
THE MATH: A board member facing a $500 small claims filing that requires them to take a day off work, hire a lawyer, respond to discovery, and defend themselves personally — while 11 of their neighbors file identical suits simultaneously — is a board member who is about to call an emergency meeting to discuss contract termination. They are not Flock Safety. They do not have a litigation war chest. They have a day job, a D&O policy with a $10,000 deductible, and a property management company that wants nothing to do with any of this.

The goal is not necessarily to win every case. The goal is to make the cost of maintaining the surveillance system exceed the cost of terminating it. Twelve organized neighbors filing individually, rotating tasks, sustaining pressure week over week — that is a machine the board cannot outlast.
